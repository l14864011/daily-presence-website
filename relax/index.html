Guided practice · 60 seconds a round

Paired muscle relaxation

Tense a muscle group hard for five seconds, then drop it all at once and notice the difference. Follow the ring — you don't need to watch the screen once you know the rhythm.

One full round through four muscle groups, with a bell at each change. It loops — let it run for as long as you're practising.

How to do it

  1. Squeeze the muscle group firmly for five seconds, breathing in.
  2. Let go all at once as you breathe out, and say the word relax to yourself.
  3. Stay with it for ten seconds and notice how different the muscle feels now.
  4. Move to the next group and repeat: face, then shoulders and hands, then stomach, then legs and feet.

The sudden release is the part that matters. Easing off slowly feels gentler but it blunts the contrast, and the contrast is the whole mechanism — it's how your body learns to recognise tension in the first place.

Before you start

  • Tense firmly, but never to the point of cramping or pain.
  • Skip any area that's injured, inflamed, or recovering.
  • Keep breathing throughout — don't hold your breath during the squeeze.
  • Sitting or lying down both work. Lying down usually goes deeper.

How often

A full session is ten to twenty minutes, so let the video loop rather than doing a single round. Practise daily for the first two weeks — ideally twice, morning and evening — then a few times a week to keep it.

It's a skill, not a one-off. You're training your body to tell tense from relaxed, and that only comes from repetition. Most people notice the difference somewhere in the second week.

Where this comes from

The tense-and-release sequence is standard progressive muscle relaxation, shortened to four muscle groups — the briefest version with research behind it. Pairing the release with the word relax comes from dialectical behaviour therapy, where it's taught as part of the TIPP distress tolerance skills.
